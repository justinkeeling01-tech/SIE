Domain 1 — Knowledge of Capital Markets 16% · 12 questions▶
Chapter 1.1 — Regulatory Entities

SEC, FINRA, MSRB & Other Regulators

SEC: Federal agency created by the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. Mission: protect investors, maintain fair/orderly markets. Has enforcement authority — can bring civil charges and refer criminal cases to DOJ. Five commissioners appointed by the President. Key divisions: Trading & Markets, Investment Management, Corporation Finance, Enforcement.

FINRA: Self-Regulatory Organization (SRO) for broker-dealers. Not a government agency — funded by industry. Writes rules, conducts exams (including this one), enforces rules, operates BrokerCheck (free public database of registered reps). FINRA rules may be stricter than SEC rules but never less strict.

MSRB: Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board. Sets rules for municipal securities market. Critical: has NO enforcement authority. FINRA enforces for broker-dealers; SEC enforces for municipal advisors. MSRB Rule G-17: fair dealing in municipal activities.

Other regulators: NASAA = state securities regulators (blue-sky laws). Federal Reserve = monetary policy and Regulation T. FDIC = bank deposits up to $250K per account. SIPC = brokerage customer accounts up to $500K. FinCEN = AML/BSA. OFAC = sanctions (SDN list). CBOE = regulates options markets.

Market participants:

  • Broker-dealers: Execute trades for clients (broker = agent, earns commission) or own account (dealer = principal, earns markup/markdown). Cannot charge both on the same trade.
  • Market makers: Always willing to buy AND sell a security; maintain bid/ask quotes; provide liquidity; profit from the spread.
  • Investment advisers: Give advice for compensation; subject to fiduciary duty.
  • Transfer agents: Maintain shareholder records; issue/cancel stock certificates.
  • Registrars: Verify company doesn't issue more shares than authorized in its charter.
  • DTCC (Depository Trust & Clearing Corp): Clears and settles most U.S. securities trades; reduces counterparty risk.
  • OCC (Options Clearing Corp): Central counterparty guaranteeing all listed options contracts.
  • Accredited investors: Net worth >$1M excluding primary home, OR income >$200K ($300K joint for last 2 years).
  • QIBs (Qualified Institutional Buyers): Institutions managing ≥$100M — eligible for Rule 144A purchases.
Chapter 1.2 — Market Structure

Primary, Secondary, Third & Fourth Markets

Primary market: New securities issued and sold for the FIRST TIME. Issuer receives proceeds. Examples: IPOs, follow-on offerings, new bond issuances, rights offerings.

Secondary market: Previously issued securities trade BETWEEN INVESTORS. Issuer receives NO proceeds from secondary trades. Examples: NYSE, NASDAQ, OTC markets. This is where virtually all daily trading occurs.

Third market: Exchange-listed securities traded OTC (off the exchange). Allows institutions to execute large block trades without moving exchange prices.

Fourth market: Direct institution-to-institution trading without broker-dealer involvement. Electronic Communication Networks (ECNs) and Alternative Trading Systems (ATSs) facilitate this.

OTC vs. Exchange: Exchanges have a central location and strict listing requirements (minimum price, revenue, shares outstanding). OTC is a decentralized dealer network. NASDAQ is technically OTC but highly regulated. OTC Bulletin Board and Pink Sheets are less regulated OTC markets.

Chapter 1.3 — Economic Factors

Federal Reserve, Business Cycles & Economic Indicators

Monetary policy (Fed) vs. Fiscal policy (Congress/President): Monetary = Federal Reserve controls money supply and interest rates. Fiscal = government spending and taxation decisions. Keynesian theory: government should increase spending in recessions. Monetarist theory: control money supply to manage inflation (Milton Friedman).

Fed tools — in order of frequency of use:

  • Open market operations (most common): Buy Treasuries = inject money = lower rates (dovish/expansionary). Sell Treasuries = remove money = raise rates (hawkish/contractionary).
  • Federal funds rate: Target rate for overnight bank-to-bank loans. The primary benchmark rate.
  • Discount rate: Rate Fed charges banks for borrowing directly from the Fed. Always higher than fed funds rate.
  • Reserve requirements: Percentage of deposits banks must hold; changed very rarely.

Hawkish Fed (raising rates): Bond prices fall → shorten bond maturities → growth stocks hurt → banks benefit → fight inflation.
Dovish Fed (lowering rates): Bond prices rise → lengthen maturities → growth stocks boosted → housing stimulated → economy stimulated.

Business cycle: Expansion → Peak → Contraction (recession = 2+ quarters of negative GDP) → Trough → Recovery. Cyclical stocks (banks, autos, materials) amplify the cycle. Defensive stocks (utilities, consumer staples, healthcare) resist downturns. Growth stocks (tech) depend on future earnings expectations and are hurt most by rising rates.

Economic indicators: Leading (predict future — stock prices, building permits, M2, manufacturing orders, jobless claims). Coincident (reflect current — GDP, employment, retail sales, personal income). Lagging (confirm trends — unemployment rate, CPI, prime rate, commercial loans).

International factors: GDP = all goods/services produced within U.S. borders. GNP = goods/services produced by U.S. citizens anywhere. Balance of payments = current account (trade in goods/services) + capital account (investment/financial flows). Trade deficit = imports exceed exports. Strong dollar = cheaper imports, harder to export.

Chapter 1.4 — Offerings

Underwriting, IPO Process & Exemptions

Underwriting types: Firm commitment = underwriter buys ALL shares from issuer at negotiated price, resells to public; bears all unsold inventory risk. Best efforts = underwriter acts as agent, sells as much as possible, returns unsold shares; issuer bears risk. All-or-none = cancelled if not fully sold. Mini-max = must sell minimum; can sell up to maximum.

IPO process: 1) File S-1 registration statement with SEC. 2) Cooling-off/waiting period (min 20 days) — SEC reviews disclosure but does NOT approve or guarantee accuracy. 3) Red herring (preliminary prospectus) distributed to gauge interest — lacks final price and effective date. 4) Road show. 5) Effective date — final pricing set, final prospectus issued. 6) Sales begin. The SEC never "approves" a security — it only reviews disclosure adequacy.

Shelf registration (Rule 415): Allows issuers to register securities in advance and sell them in tranches over up to 3 years as market conditions allow.

Exempt offerings (don't need full SEC registration):

  • Reg D (private placements): Rule 506(b): up to 35 non-accredited investors + unlimited accredited, no general solicitation. Rule 506(c): unlimited accredited investors only, general solicitation allowed but must verify accreditation.
  • Rule 144: Governs resale of restricted securities (from Reg D) and control securities (held by corporate insiders). Subject to holding periods, volume limits, and filing requirements.
  • Rule 144A: Resale of restricted securities to QIBs without registration. Creates liquidity in private placement market.
  • Rule 147 (intrastate): Issuer and ALL purchasers must be in same state. No interstate resales for 9 months. Issuer must be incorporated and do significant business in that state.
  • Regulation A+: Smaller public offerings up to $75M, streamlined process ("mini-IPO").

Blue-sky laws: State securities laws. Must comply with both federal and applicable state laws. States may be MORE restrictive. Coordinated by NASAA.

Domain 2 — Understanding Products & Their Risks 44% · 33 questions▶
Chapter 2.1 — Equity Securities

Common Stock, Preferred Stock, ADRs, Rights & Warrants

Common stock rights: Vote (1 share = 1 vote on major corporate matters), receive dividends (NOT guaranteed — board's discretion), preemptive right (maintain ownership % in new share offerings), residual claim in liquidation (LAST in line after all creditors and preferred shareholders). Limited liability — max loss = investment.

Stock splits: 2:1 forward split → shares double, price halves, total market cap unchanged, cost basis per share halves. Reverse split → shares decrease, price increases (used to avoid delisting for stocks below $1). Neither changes stockholder's proportional ownership or total value.

Preferred stock types (all have priority over common for dividends and liquidation):

  • Cumulative: Missed dividends ("arrears") accumulate; ALL must be paid before common gets anything. Most common type tested.
  • Non-cumulative: Missed dividends are gone permanently.
  • Convertible: Can convert to common stock at set conversion ratio; price influenced by both interest rates and common stock price.
  • Callable: Issuer can redeem at call price; issuer calls when rates fall (bad for investor — must reinvest at lower rates).
  • Participating: May receive additional dividends above stated rate if company profits allow.
  • Adjustable-rate: Dividend rate floats based on benchmark rate.

ADRs (American Depositary Receipts): Represent ownership in foreign company shares. Trade on U.S. exchanges in U.S. dollars. Subject to currency risk. Dividends paid in USD after converting from foreign currency. Sponsored = company participates in creation; Unsponsored = bank-created without company involvement.

Rights vs. Warrants: Rights = short-term (30–45 days), issued to existing shareholders, allow purchase of new shares at discount. Warrants = long-term (years), issued by company (often as bond sweeteners), allow purchase at set strike price. Neither is the same as exchange-listed options (which are OCC-issued).

Liquidation priority order (memorize): 1) Secured creditors (mortgage/equipment bond holders) → 2) Senior unsecured debenture holders → 3) Subordinated debenture holders → 4) Preferred stockholders → 5) Common stockholders (usually get nothing).

Chapter 2.2 — Debt Securities

Bonds: Treasury, Corporate, Municipal & Agency Securities

THE most important bond concept: BOND PRICES AND YIELDS ALWAYS MOVE IN OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS. Rising rates → existing bond prices fall (new bonds pay more, so old bonds become less attractive). Falling rates → existing bond prices rise.

Bond basics: Par value = $1,000. Coupon rate = annual interest ÷ par. Most bonds pay semi-annual interest. Duration = measure of interest rate sensitivity (longer duration = more price change per rate move).

Five yield measures (all tested):

  • Coupon rate: Fixed — never changes regardless of price
  • Current yield: Annual interest ($) ÷ current market price. Formula: CY = Annual Coupon / Price
  • YTM (Yield to Maturity): Total return if held to maturity — accounts for coupon income + discount/premium amortized over life. Most comprehensive yield. Equals coupon rate ONLY when bond trades at par.
  • YTC (Yield to Call): Return if bond called at the first call date
  • YTW (Yield to Worst): Lower of YTM or YTC — the worst possible return for the investor

Yield ordering (critical): Premium bond (price > $1,000): Coupon > Current Yield > YTM > YTC. Discount bond (price < $1,000): Coupon < Current Yield < YTM < YTC.

U.S. Treasury securities: T-bills (4–52 weeks, issued at DISCOUNT, no coupon, most liquid security in world). T-notes (2–10 years, semi-annual coupon). T-bonds (10–30 years, semi-annual coupon). TIPS: principal adjusts with CPI — great inflation hedge; "real" yield is fixed, nominal yield fluctuates. Series EE: purchased at discount, double in 20 years; tax-deferred until redemption; exempt from state/local taxes. All Treasury securities = lowest default risk. NOT free of interest rate risk.

Agency securities: GNMA = FULL faith and credit U.S. government guarantee. FNMA and FHLMC = GSEs with only "implicit" support — NOT a direct guarantee. All issue mortgage-backed securities (MBS). Subject to PREPAYMENT RISK: homeowners refinance when rates fall, returning principal early; investor must reinvest at lower rates.

Corporate bonds by seniority: Secured (mortgage bonds, equipment trust certificates) → Senior unsecured debentures → Subordinated debentures → Convertible bonds → Preferred stock → Common stock.

Zero-coupon bonds: Deep discount, no periodic payments, face value at maturity. No reinvestment risk. Phantom income = IRS taxes the annual accretion even though no cash received. More interest rate risk than coupon bonds of same maturity (entire return received at end).

Municipal bonds: GO bonds: backed by taxing power of municipality — safest muni type, voter-approved. Revenue bonds: backed ONLY by specific project revenues (tolls, hospital fees, utility revenue) — higher risk; if project fails, no taxing power to fall back on. Interest federally tax-exempt (usually state/local exempt if issued in your state). Taxable equivalent yield = Muni yield ÷ (1 − tax bracket). Day-count convention: 30/360. Official statement = muni prospectus. Munis are EXEMPT from SEC registration. Short-term munis: TANs (Tax Anticipation Notes), RANs (Revenue ANs), BANs (Bond ANs). Never put munis in an IRA/401(k) — wastes the tax-exempt benefit on an already tax-deferred account.

Bond ratings: Investment grade: S&P BBB- or above / Moody's Baa3 or above. Below = speculative/high-yield/junk. Rating agencies: Moody's, S&P, Fitch. Higher rating = lower yield (less risk = less compensation needed). Unrated ≠ automatically junk.

Chapter 2.3 — Options

Calls, Puts, Strategies, OCC & Options Disclosure

Options basics: 1 contract = 100 shares. Buyer (long position) pays PREMIUM for the RIGHT (but not obligation). Seller/writer (short position) receives premium and has OBLIGATION to perform if buyer exercises. Premium = Intrinsic Value + Time Value. Time value erodes to zero at expiration (theta decay).

Call option: RIGHT to BUY 100 shares at strike price. In the money: stock price > strike. Bullish strategy. Long call: max loss = premium paid. Max gain = unlimited.

Put option: RIGHT to SELL 100 shares at strike price. In the money: stock price < strike. Bearish strategy. Long put: max loss = premium paid. Max gain = strike − premium (if stock goes to zero).

Moneyness: In the money (ITM): has intrinsic value. At the money (ATM): stock = strike, no intrinsic value. Out of the money (OTM): no intrinsic value, only time value.

Key strategies:

  • Covered call: Own 100 shares + sell (write) a call. Generates premium income. Caps upside above strike. "Covered" because you can deliver the shares.
  • Protective put (married put): Own 100 shares + buy a put. Insurance against price decline. Establishes a floor price. Max loss = (stock purchase price − strike price + premium).
  • Long straddle: Buy call + buy put at same strike/expiration. Profits from big move in EITHER direction. Loss if stock stays flat (lose both premiums).
  • Long call break-even: Strike price + premium paid.
  • Long put break-even: Strike price − premium paid.

American vs. European: American = exercise any time before expiration. European = exercise ONLY at expiration. Most U.S. equity options = American. Most index options = European (cash settled).

OCC: Options Clearing Corporation acts as central counterparty for all listed options — guarantees performance of every standardized contract, eliminating individual counterparty risk.

ODD (Options Disclosure Document): "Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options" — MUST be delivered at or before the time of the customer's FIRST options transaction. Required by FINRA/OCC rules.

Reg T and options: Options PURCHASES must be paid in full — margin cannot be used to BUY options. Writing (selling) options may require margin as collateral for the obligation. Regulation T does not permit margin for option contracts as a general rule.

Chapter 2.4 — Packaged Products

Mutual Funds, ETFs, Closed-End Funds, UITs & Variable Annuities

Open-end mutual funds: NAV = (Total Assets − Liabilities) ÷ Shares Outstanding. Calculated ONCE daily at 4:00 PM ET market close. All orders placed before 4PM get that day's NAV. Cannot be shorted or bought on margin. Shares redeemed at NAV (no secondary market).

Share classes: Class A: front-end load, lower annual expenses — best for long-term. Formula: Sales charge % = (POP − NAV) ÷ POP. Class B: back-end/CDSC load charged at redemption, decreases each year, eventually converts to A shares, higher annual expenses. Class C: level load (~1% annual 12b-1 fee), no front-end load, doesn't convert to A — best for short holding periods. No-load: no sales charges; 12b-1 ≤0.25%.

12b-1 fees: Annual distribution/marketing fees charged from fund assets. Max 1% annually. A fund with 12b-1 ≤0.25% and no other sales charges may still be called "no-load."

Breakpoints and LOI: Breakpoints = investment thresholds that reduce Class A load. FINRA prohibits "breakpoint selling" (intentionally selling just below threshold to maximize commission). LOI (Letter of Intent): customer commits to reach a breakpoint over 13 months — gets reduced load now; if they don't invest the full amount, fund retroactively charges higher load. ROA (Right of Accumulation): existing fund value counts toward breakpoint threshold.

Closed-end funds: Fixed shares issued in IPO; trade on exchange at MARKET PRICE determined by supply/demand — can trade at PREMIUM or DISCOUNT to NAV. Can be bought on margin and sold short.

ETFs: Trade continuously on exchange like stocks. Price close to NAV due to creation/redemption mechanism (arbitrage keeps ETF price aligned with underlying). Usually passively managed. Lower expense ratios. Can be shorted and margined. ETNs = debt obligation of issuing bank, linked to index, carries issuer credit risk (unlike ETFs that hold actual securities).

UITs (Unit Investment Trusts): Fixed, unmanaged portfolio. No ongoing management decisions. Issues redeemable units. Terminates on a set date. Lower costs than actively managed funds.

Variable annuities: Insurance product AND security — requires securities AND insurance license to sell. Invested in SEPARATE ACCOUNT (legally segregated from insurer's general assets — creditor-protected). Owner bears ALL investment risk. Accumulation phase (buy accumulation units) → Annuitization (convert to annuity units for lifetime/period-certain income payments). Death benefit = typically greater of account value or total premiums paid. Surrender charges apply for early withdrawal (typically 7–8 year schedule). 1035 exchange = tax-free swap of one annuity for another. Fixed annuity = guaranteed rate, general account, insurance product only — NO securities license needed.

Chapter 2.5 — Municipal Fund Securities

529 Plans, ABLE Accounts & Local Government Investment Pools

529 Plans (two types): Prepaid tuition plans: lock in today's tuition rates at eligible schools. College savings plans: invest in mutual fund-like sub-accounts; value fluctuates with markets. Contributions NOT federally deductible; earnings grow tax-free; withdrawals tax-free if used for qualified education expenses. Account OWNER controls assets (not the beneficiary). Can change beneficiary to another family member without penalty.

ABLE Accounts: Tax-advantaged savings for individuals with qualifying disabilities that began before age 26. Annual contribution limit ~$18,000 (gift tax exclusion amount). Earnings grow tax-free; withdrawals tax-free for "qualified disability expenses." Account balance up to $100,000 doesn't disqualify from SSI. Regulated as municipal fund securities under MSRB Rule G-45.

LGIPs (Local Government Investment Pools): State-operated pools for local governments to invest excess cash. Similar to money market funds. NOT available to retail investors. Managed per state law.

Chapter 2.6 — Alternative Investments

DPPs, REITs, Hedge Funds & Private Equity

DPPs (Direct Participation Programs): Pass-through of income AND losses directly to investors. Types: real estate LPs, oil and gas, equipment leasing, agriculture. Key features: generally ILLIQUID (no exchange listing), long lock-up periods, pass-through tax treatment (losses reduce investor's ordinary income — big draw), general partner manages day-to-day, limited partners are PASSIVE (cannot participate in management without losing limited liability). DPP interests are securities and must be registered unless exempt.

REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts): Must distribute ≥90% of taxable income as dividends to maintain REIT status (avoids corporate-level tax). Types: Equity REITs (own/operate properties), Mortgage REITs (hold mortgages/MBS), Hybrid. Listed REITs = highly liquid. Non-traded REITs = illiquid, high fees. Private REITs = accredited investors only, very illiquid. Subject to interest rate risk (rising rates hurt REIT prices).

Hedge funds: Private, unregistered partnerships exempt from Investment Company Act 1940. Accredited/qualified purchasers only. Illiquid with lock-up periods (often 1–2 years). Use leverage, short selling, derivatives, complex strategies. Typical fee: "2 and 20" (2% annual management + 20% of profits). NOT required to publish daily NAV.

Chapter 2.7 — Risk Types

All 11 Investment Risk Types — Know Every One

Market/systematic risk: Broad market decline affecting virtually all securities. Cannot be eliminated by diversification. Measured by beta. Example: 2008 financial crisis.

Non-systematic (specific/unsystematic) risk: Company or industry-specific. CAN be reduced by diversification across different securities/sectors.

Credit/default risk: Issuer fails to make scheduled interest or principal payments. Highest for junk bonds; essentially zero for U.S. Treasuries.

Interest rate risk: Rising rates cause bond prices to fall. Longer maturity/duration = greater price sensitivity. ALL fixed-rate bonds have this risk.

Reinvestment risk: Periodic coupon payments reinvested at lower rates when interest rates decline. Zero-coupon bonds have ZERO reinvestment risk (no coupons). Callable bonds have highest reinvestment risk.

Call risk: Issuer calls bond before maturity when rates fall — precisely when investor wants to keep earning the high coupon. Investor forced to reinvest at lower rates.

Inflation (purchasing power) risk: Investment returns don't keep pace with inflation; real return is negative. Greatest long-term threat for fixed-income investors. TIPS and equities hedge inflation.

Liquidity risk: Cannot sell investment quickly at a fair price. Highest: DPPs, non-traded REITs, private equity. Lowest: T-bills.

Currency risk: Foreign investments lose value due to exchange rate movements. Only affects investments denominated in foreign currencies.

Political/legislative risk: Government actions (laws, regulations, taxes) negatively impact investments. Example: drug pricing legislation hurts pharmaceutical stocks.

Prepayment risk: MBS-specific. Homeowners refinance/pay off mortgages early when rates fall — principal returned sooner than expected; must reinvest at lower rates.

Domain 3 — Trading, Customer Accounts & Prohibited Activities 31% · 23 questions▶
Chapter 3.1 — Order Types

Market, Limit, Stop, Stop-Limit & Time Qualifiers

Market order: Execute immediately at best available price. EXECUTION guaranteed; PRICE not. Use for liquid securities when speed matters more than exact price.

Limit order: Execute only at specified price or BETTER. Buy limit = at limit or lower. Sell limit = at limit or higher. PRICE guaranteed; EXECUTION not. May never fill if price isn't reached.

Stop order (stop-loss): Placed AWAY from current market. When triggered at stop price → becomes MARKET ORDER (executes at next available price, which may be worse than stop in volatile markets). Buy stop: above current price (short seller protection). Sell stop: below current price (protect profits/limit losses).

Stop-limit order: When triggered at stop price → becomes LIMIT ORDER. Provides price protection but may NOT execute if price gaps past the limit. More control, less certainty.

Time qualifiers: Day order: expires unfilled at end of trading day. GTC: open until filled or customer cancels. FOK (Fill or Kill): fill entire order immediately or cancel all. IOC (Immediate or Cancel): fill what's available now, cancel rest. AON (All-or-None): fill complete order only, may wait.

Discretionary accounts: Registered rep has written authority to choose security, price, and timing without per-trade customer approval. Must be authorized in writing BEFORE trading. Extra principal supervision required.

Principal vs. agency capacity: Agency = BD acts as agent for customer, charges commission. Principal = BD trades from own inventory, charges markup/markdown. CANNOT charge both commission AND markup on the same transaction. Must disclose capacity on confirmation.

Chapter 3.2 — Settlement & Corporate Actions

Settlement Dates, Dividends & Corporate Actions

Current settlement dates (MUST know): Equities (stocks): T+1 (effective May 28, 2024). U.S. Treasuries: T+1. Options: T+1. Corporate bonds: T+2. Municipal bonds: T+2. Mutual fund redemptions: T+1 (typically).

Dividend timeline (sequence): 1) Declaration date: board announces. 2) Ex-dividend date: first day buyer does NOT get dividend; with T+1, ex-date = 1 business day before record date. 3) Record date: must appear on shareholder books. 4) Payment date: dividend paid; this is the TAX EVENT (taxable in the year paid).

Corporate actions: Forward stock split (2:1): shares double, price halves, cost basis per share halves, total value unchanged. Reverse split: shares decrease, price increases (avoid delisting). Cash buyback/tender offer: company buys own shares, reduces shares outstanding, increases EPS. Rights offering: existing shareholders buy new shares at discount within ~30–45 days. Spin-off: company divides, shareholders receive new entity shares (usually tax-free). Merger/acquisition: combines two companies; requires board and shareholder approval.

Indices: S&P 500: 500 large-cap U.S. stocks, market-cap weighted. DJIA: 30 blue-chip stocks, PRICE-WEIGHTED (higher-priced stocks have more influence). NASDAQ Composite: tech-heavy, market-cap weighted. Russell 2000: small-cap benchmark. Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index: investment-grade bond benchmark.

Chapter 3.3 — Margin Accounts

Regulation T, FINRA Maintenance & Margin Calculations

Regulation T (Federal Reserve — initial margin): 50% for long stock purchases AND short sales. Applies to both sides of margin. The broker lends the remaining 50% (debit balance).

FINRA Rule 4210 (maintenance margin): Long positions: 25% minimum equity. Short positions: 30% minimum equity. Firms may set HIGHER "house" requirements but NEVER lower. Below maintenance → margin call.

Minimum to open margin account: $2,000 (FINRA rule). No credit extended below this even if Reg T would require less.

Key formulas: Equity = Market Value − Debit Balance. Equity % = Equity ÷ Market Value. Maintenance requirement = Market Value × 25%. Margin call when Equity < Market Value × 25%.

Margin call process: Broker issues call requiring deposit of cash or marginable securities. If not met, broker may sell securities WITHOUT PRIOR NOTICE to restore the account. Customer cannot specify which securities are sold.

Options and margin: Options PURCHASES must be paid in full (Reg T prohibits margin on long options). Writing options typically requires margin deposit as collateral. Pattern Day Trader: 4+ day trades in 5 business days → must maintain $25,000 minimum equity.

Free-riding (cash accounts): Buying and selling a security before full payment is received. PROHIBITED under Reg T. Penalty: account frozen 90 days (must have full payment available before placing future orders).

Chapter 3.4 — Customer Account Types

Individual, Joint, Custodial, Retirement & Institutional Accounts

Individual account: Single owner makes all decisions. Assets go to estate at death per will or law.

Joint accounts: JTWROS (Joint Tenants With Right of Survivorship): deceased's share automatically passes to surviving owner(s) OUTSIDE of probate — by operation of law. Most common joint account type. TIC (Tenants in Common): deceased's share passes to their ESTATE per their will — NOT to the survivor. Each owner can will their share to different beneficiaries.

Custodial accounts (UGMA/UTMA): ONE custodian manages for ONE minor beneficiary. Gift is IRREVOCABLE once made — cannot be taken back. Assets legally belong to the minor; custodian manages until majority. UGMA: cash, stocks, bonds, mutual funds. UTMA: any type of property including real estate, royalties. UTMA typically vests at 21 (vs UGMA at 18 in many states). Income taxed at minor's rate (kiddie tax may apply).

Trust accounts: Revocable trust: grantor can modify/revoke. Irrevocable trust: cannot be changed — provides estate tax benefits. Trustee has fiduciary duty to beneficiaries per trust document.

Retirement accounts: Traditional IRA: contributions may be tax-deductible; growth tax-deferred; withdrawals taxed as ordinary income; 10% penalty for withdrawals before 59½; RMDs begin at age 73. Roth IRA: contributions NOT deductible; growth TAX-FREE; qualified withdrawals tax-free; NO RMDs during owner's lifetime. SEP-IRA: for self-employed, higher contribution limits. 401(k): employer-sponsored, pre-tax contributions, possible employer match. 403(b): non-profit/educational employees.

KYC — FINRA Rule 2090: Must collect and maintain: name, address, date of birth, SSN/TIN, employment status, financial situation, investment objectives, and risk tolerance. Must update when material changes occur. Beneficial ownership rule: for entity accounts, must identify all individuals owning ≥25% plus one control person.

POA (Power of Attorney): Limited = specific powers (trade execution only). Full/Durable = broad authority including withdrawals. Durable continues if grantor becomes incapacitated. Must be on file with broker-dealer before acting on it.

Chapter 3.5 — AML & Privacy

Bank Secrecy Act, CTR, SAR, Structuring, OFAC & Reg S-P

Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) of 1970: Foundation of U.S. AML law. All financial institutions including broker-dealers must maintain AML compliance programs. Enforced by FinCEN. Required elements: written policies/procedures, designated AML compliance officer, ongoing employee training, independent testing/audit.

Three stages of money laundering: 1) PLACEMENT: introducing illegal cash into the financial system. 2) LAYERING: moving money through multiple transactions to obscure origin. 3) INTEGRATION: reintroducing "cleaned" money into the economy as legitimate funds.

CTR (Currency Transaction Report): Required for any CASH transaction exceeding $10,000. Filed by the institution with FinCEN. MANDATORY — no judgment about whether suspicious. Covers deposits, withdrawals, currency exchange, monetary instrument purchases.

SAR (Suspicious Activity Report): Required for suspicious transactions ≥$5,000. Filed with FinCEN within 30 days of detection (60 days if no suspect identified). CONFIDENTIAL — MUST NOT inform the customer a SAR was filed. Failure to file is itself a crime. The SAR is NOT an accusation of a crime.

Structuring (smurfing): Intentionally breaking up transactions to avoid the $10,000 CTR threshold. ILLEGAL under BSA even if the underlying funds are perfectly legitimate. Example: $9,400 deposits on consecutive days.

USA PATRIOT Act: Post-9/11 AML enhancements. Section 326: CIP (Customer Identification Program) — verify identity of ALL new customers: name, address, DOB, identification number. Section 352: AML programs required for all broker-dealers.

OFAC (Office of Foreign Assets Control): Administers U.S. economic and trade sanctions. Maintains SDN (Specially Designated Nationals) list. Broker-dealers MUST screen ALL customers against SDN list before opening accounts. Cannot do business with anyone on the list.

Regulation S-P (Privacy of Consumer Financial Information): Protects customers' nonpublic personal information. Initial privacy notice required at start of relationship. Annual privacy notice required. Opt-out right: customers can opt out of information sharing with non-affiliated third parties. Safeguard requirements: must protect customer data from unauthorized access and breaches.

Books and records retention: Customer account records: 6 years (3 years in accessible location). Order tickets/trade blotters: 3 years. Partnership agreements, articles of incorporation: lifetime. Business Continuity Plans (BCPs): must be maintained and updated per FINRA Rule 4370.

Chapter 3.6 — Suitability & Reg BI

Know Your Customer, Regulation Best Interest & Communications

Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI — SEC Rule 15l-1): Effective June 30, 2020. Applies to broker-dealers making recommendations to RETAIL CUSTOMERS ONLY. Four obligations: 1) Disclosure: provide Form CRS. 2) Care: exercise diligence, care, and skill; act in customer's BEST INTEREST. 3) Conflict of interest: identify, disclose, and mitigate conflicts. 4) Compliance: establish policies and procedures.

FINRA Suitability Rule 2111: Still applies to institutional customers (not covered by Reg BI). Three dimensions: reasonable-basis suitability (product suitable for at least some customers), customer-specific suitability (suitable for THIS customer's profile), quantitative suitability (frequency of trading is reasonable for this account).

Form CRS: 2-page standardized "Customer Relationship Summary." Must be delivered at start of relationship. Covers: services offered, fees and costs, conflicts of interest, legal standard of care (BD vs IA), disciplinary history. Required for both broker-dealers and investment advisers.

FINRA Rule 2210 — Communications with the public: Retail communications (advertisements, social media to public): must be pre-approved by registered principal before use. Institutional communications (to institutions only): no pre-approval required but must be reviewed. Correspondence (individual customer communications): no pre-approval required but subject to supervision. All communications: must be fair, balanced, not misleading; no predictions of future performance; no omission of material facts.

Telemarketing rules (FINRA Rule 3230 / FTC Telemarketing Sales Rule): Must maintain and honor Do-Not-Call list. No calls before 8 AM or after 9 PM (customer's local time). Must disclose caller's name and firm. No recorded messages without consent.

Chapter 3.7 — Prohibited Activities

Complete List: Insider Trading, Market Manipulation & Other Violations

Insider trading: Trading on Material Non-Public Information (MNPI) = information a reasonable investor would consider significant AND that hasn't been publicly released. Both TIPPER (person sharing MNPI) and TIPPEE (person trading on it) are liable. Criminal penalties: up to 20 years prison + $5M individual fines. Civil penalties: up to 3× profit gained or loss avoided. Misappropriation theory: using confidential information in breach of a duty (even non-insiders can commit insider trading).

Market manipulation types (all prohibited):

  • Front-running: Trading own/firm account ahead of large customer orders using knowledge of those pending orders. FINRA Rule 5270/5320.
  • Trading ahead of research reports: Buying/selling before firm publishes research that will move the price.
  • Pump and dump: Spreading false positive information to inflate price, then selling at the top.
  • Painting the tape / wash trading: Offsetting transactions between related parties to create false appearance of volume/activity.
  • Marking the close: Trades at end of day to artificially influence closing price (used for portfolio marking).
  • Marking the open: Trades at market open to set artificial opening price.
  • Backing away: Market maker refuses to honor a firm published quote.
  • Spoofing: Placing orders with intent to cancel before execution to create false demand/supply signals.
  • Capping: Preventing a security's price from rising above a certain level.
  • Pegging: Artificially maintaining a price at a specific level.

Other prohibited activities:

  • Churning: Excessive trading primarily to generate commissions. Violates FINRA Rule 2111.
  • Selling away: Selling products not offered by employing firm without firm's written approval (FINRA Rule 3280).
  • Unauthorized trading: Executing trades in non-discretionary accounts without customer authorization.
  • Sharing in customer accounts: Prohibited UNLESS: written approval from BOTH customer and firm, AND sharing is proportional to investment contribution. RR cannot share based solely on performance.
  • Borrowing from/lending to customers: Prohibited except with specific firm approval for qualified relationships (financial institutions, family members who support the RR) per FINRA Rule 3240.
  • Guaranteeing against losses: ABSOLUTELY PROHIBITED. Never promise customers they won't lose money.
  • Financial exploitation of seniors (FINRA Rule 2165): Firms MAY place temporary hold on disbursements if exploitation suspected. Must notify trusted contact person on file.
  • IPO restrictions (FINRA Rule 5130): Registered persons and immediate family members CANNOT buy IPOs at offering price (limits who can get "hot" IPOs).
  • Signatures of convenience: Signing customer's name on documents without authorization = prohibited.
  • Paying commissions to unregistered persons: Prohibited under FINRA Rule 2040.

Chapter 4.1 — Securities Laws

The 5 Key Laws Every SIE Candidate Must Know

Securities Act of 1933 ("Truth in Securities Act" / "Paper Act"): Governs the PRIMARY MARKET — new securities offerings. Requires registration with SEC and delivery of a prospectus with full material disclosure. Aims to ensure investors receive accurate information before buying. Imposes strict liability for material misstatements in registration statements. The SEC reviews disclosure but does NOT approve or guarantee accuracy of securities — RRs cannot say securities are "SEC-approved."

Securities Exchange Act of 1934 ("Exchange Act"): Created the SEC. Governs the SECONDARY market — trading in already-issued securities. Regulates broker-dealers, exchanges, and ongoing reporting by public companies (10-K annual, 10-Q quarterly, 8-K current event). Prohibits insider trading (Section 10(b), Rule 10b-5). Established registration requirements for securities on exchanges.

Investment Company Act of 1940: Regulates investment companies — open-end mutual funds, closed-end funds, ETFs, UITs. Requires SEC registration and regular disclosure. Requires majority of independent directors. Limits leverage. Requires daily NAV calculation for open-end funds.

Investment Advisers Act of 1940: Regulates investment advisers. Requires SEC registration for advisers managing ≥$110M AUM (smaller IAs register with states). Establishes FIDUCIARY STANDARD — advisers MUST always put client's interests ahead of their own at all times. Prohibits performance fees for retail clients (except qualified clients). Requires written advisory agreements.

SIPC Act of 1970 (Securities Investor Protection Act): Created SIPC. Protects customer assets if broker-dealer FAILS (insolvency). Coverage: up to $500,000 per customer, with $250,000 maximum for cash claims. Separate coverage per account type (individual, IRA, joint — each gets up to $500K). Does NOT cover: market losses, investment fraud, commodity futures, currencies, or investment company securities not yet purchased. All FINRA member firms must be SIPC members. SIPC ≠ FDIC (FDIC is for banks; SIPC is for brokerage accounts).

Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX) 2002: Response to Enron/WorldCom. CEO/CFO must personally certify financial statements (criminal liability for false certifications). Created PCAOB (Public Company Accounting Oversight Board) to oversee auditors. Increased criminal penalties for corporate fraud. Required audit committees composed of independent directors.

Key principle: SRO rules (FINRA, MSRB) may be MORE restrictive than SEC rules but NEVER less restrictive.

Chapter 4.2 — Registration & Continuing Education

Form U4/U5, Licensing Requirements & Employee Conduct

Who must register: Any "associated person" at a FINRA member firm who engages in or supervises securities activities. Unregistered persons CANNOT: solicit customers, take orders, give investment advice, or receive transaction-based compensation.

Exam structure: SIE + appropriate top-off exam = registration. Series 6: investment company/variable products. Series 7: general securities representative. Series 57: equity trader. Series 79: investment banking. Series 63/65/66: state law requirements. Must be associated with a member firm to take top-off exams (but NOT to take the SIE).

Form U4: Uniform Application for Securities Industry Registration. Filed by firm on behalf of associated person. Must be updated within 30 DAYS of any material change including: felony charges/convictions (not just convictions), financial-related misdemeanors, bankruptcy, tax liens ≥$2,500, customer complaints, regulatory actions, civil judgments. Failure to disclose = serious violation under FINRA Rule 1122.

Form U5: Uniform Termination Notice. Filed when associated person terminates employment (resign, terminated, retire). Must be filed within 30 days. Defamatory or false information on U5 can be legally challenged by the former employee.

Continuing Education: REGULATORY ELEMENT (FINRA-administered): must complete within 1 year of initial registration, then every 3 years thereafter. Online computer-based training. Covers compliance, regulatory, ethical, and sales practice standards. If not completed on time → registration is suspended. FIRM ELEMENT (firm-administered): annual requirement. Firm designs based on its specific business activities and an annual needs analysis. Tailored to firm's products and customer types.

Statutory disqualification: Bars a person from FINRA member firm association. Triggers: felony convictions within 10 years, certain financial misdemeanor convictions, SEC/FINRA disciplinary sanctions, false statements on Form U4. May apply for a waiver to continue association.

Outside business activities (FINRA Rule 3270): Must give prior WRITTEN NOTICE to employing firm before engaging in any outside business activity. Firm may restrict or prohibit. Different from private securities transactions (Rule 3280 — selling away).

Reportable events: Written customer complaints, regulatory investigations, bankruptcies, liens, arrests/criminal charges. All must be disclosed on Form U4 within 30 days.

Gifts and gratuities (FINRA Rule 3220): Maximum $100 per person per year to employees of other FINRA members. Non-cash compensation (entertainment, meals, gifts): must be reasonable, not lavish, and not condition on sales targets.

Political contributions (MSRB Rule G-37): Limits broker-dealer contributions to municipal securities officials with power to influence muni business. "Pay to play" restrictions. Exceeding limits can result in 2-year ban on negotiated muni securities business with that issuer.

Bond yield order — premium vs. discount

Premium bond (price > $1,000): Coupon Rate > Current Yield > YTM > YTC

Discount bond (price < $1,000): Coupon Rate < Current Yield < YTM < YTC

Memory: for premiums ALL yields fall below coupon. For discounts ALL yields rise above coupon. At par all yields equal the coupon.
