Domain 1 — Knowledge of Capital Markets
16% · ~12 questions▶
Primary vs. Secondary Markets

Primary market: issuer sells NEW securities to investors (IPOs, follow-on offerings). Underwriters facilitate. Secondary market: investors trade ALREADY-ISSUED securities among themselves (NYSE, NASDAQ, OTC). Issuer receives no proceeds from secondary trades.

Market Participants

Broker-dealers (buy/sell for clients and own account). Market makers (always willing to buy/sell, profit from bid-ask spread). Transfer agents (maintain shareholder records, issue/cancel certificates). Registrars (ensure shares don't exceed authorized amounts). Clearing corporations like DTCC (settle trades, reduce counterparty risk).

Underwriting Types

Firm commitment: underwriter buys ALL shares from issuer, resells to public — assumes all risk. Best efforts: underwriter sells what it can, returns unsold shares — issuer bears risk. All-or-none: offering cancelled if not fully sold. Mini-max: offering needs minimum sold to proceed.

Economic Indicators & Fed Policy

Business cycle: expansion → peak → contraction (recession) → trough → recovery. Fed tools: open market operations (most common — buy = loosen, sell = tighten), discount rate, reserve requirements. Hawkish Fed = raising rates (fight inflation). Dovish Fed = lowering rates (stimulate economy). Rising rates → bond prices fall, shorter maturities preferred.

Offering Documents

Prospectus: required disclosure document for registered offerings. Red herring (preliminary prospectus): distributed during cooling-off period, lacks final price/date. Final prospectus: must be delivered at or before sale. Official statement: muni bond equivalent of a prospectus. Reg D prospectus: private placement document.

Regulation D & Exempt Offerings

Reg D: exempts private placements from full SEC registration. Accredited investors only (net worth >$1M excluding home, OR income >$200k/$300k joint). Rule 144: governs resale of restricted/control securities. Rule 147: intrastate offerings exempt (issuer and buyers in same state). Rule 144A: allows resale of restricted securities to QIBs.

Money Market Instruments

T-bills (91-day, 182-day, 52-week — issued at discount). Commercial paper (unsecured, corporate, max 270 days). Bankers' acceptances (trade finance, 9-month max). Negotiable CDs (bank-issued, $100k+ denominations). Federal funds (overnight bank-to-bank loans at fed funds rate). Repos (repurchase agreements — overnight secured lending).

Domain 2 — Understanding Products & Their Risks
44% · ~33 questions▶
Common Stock

Ownership in a corporation. Rights: vote on major issues (one share = one vote), receive dividends (not guaranteed), residual claim in liquidation (last in line). Limited liability — can't lose more than invested. Preemptive rights let existing shareholders maintain ownership percentage in new offerings. Stock splits: 2:1 split → shares double, price halves, no change in value.

Preferred Stock

Fixed dividend, priority over common in dividends and liquidation. Typically no voting rights. Types: Cumulative (missed dividends accumulate, must all be paid before common gets anything). Non-cumulative (missed = gone). Convertible (can convert to common at set ratio). Callable (issuer can redeem). Participating (may receive extra dividends above stated rate). Par value typically $100.

Bond Fundamentals

Par value = $1,000. Coupon = annual interest rate × par. Price and yield move OPPOSITE directions (key relationship). Premium bond: price > par → YTM < coupon. Discount bond: price < par → YTM > coupon. Current yield = annual interest / market price. YTM accounts for price discount/premium amortized to maturity. YTC = yield if called at first call date. YTW (yield to worst) = lowest possible yield.

U.S. Government Securities

T-bills: 4–52 weeks, issued at discount (no coupon), most liquid. T-notes: 2–10 years, semi-annual coupon. T-bonds: 10–30 years, semi-annual coupon. TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities): principal adjusts with CPI. Series EE bonds: sold at discount, double in 20 years. Agency securities: GNMA (govt-backed), FNMA/FHLMC (GSEs, not direct govt guarantee). All backed by U.S. government = lowest default risk.

Corporate Bonds

Senior secured (backed by collateral, paid first in default). Senior unsecured debentures (no collateral, still senior). Subordinated debentures (paid after senior unsecured). Convertible bonds (can convert to stock, carry lower coupon). Zero-coupon bonds (issued at deep discount, no periodic interest, phantom income taxed annually). Investment grade: BBB-/Baa3 or above. Below = speculative/junk/high-yield.

Municipal Bonds

General Obligation (GO): backed by taxing power of municipality — safest muni. Revenue bonds: backed only by revenues from specific project (hospital, toll road, utility) — higher risk than GO. Interest is exempt from federal income tax (usually exempt from state/local tax if you live in issuing state). Not appropriate for tax-exempt accounts (IRAs, 401ks). Equivalent taxable yield = muni yield ÷ (1 - tax bracket). Munis use 30/360 day-count convention. Official statement = muni prospectus.

Mutual Funds (Open-End Investment Companies)

NAV = (Total Assets − Liabilities) ÷ shares outstanding. Calculated once daily at market close (4pm ET). Shares redeemed at NAV (no secondary trading). Load = sales charge. Class A: front-end load (charged at purchase, lower ongoing expenses — best for long-term). Class B: back-end/CDSC load (charged at redemption, converts to A after several years). Class C: level load (annual fee ~1%, no front-end, best for short term). No-load funds: sold direct, no sales charge. 12b-1 fees: ongoing distribution/marketing fees charged annually.

ETFs & Closed-End Funds

ETFs: trade on exchange like stocks, continuously priced throughout day. Usually passively managed (index-tracking). Lower expense ratios than mutual funds. Can be bought on margin, sold short. Closed-end funds: fixed number of shares, trade on exchange. Price can trade at premium or DISCOUNT to NAV (unlike mutual funds). UITs (Unit Investment Trusts): fixed, unmanaged portfolio, no ongoing management, dissolves at set date.

Options Basics

Call option: RIGHT to BUY 100 shares at strike price. Put option: RIGHT to SELL 100 shares at strike price. Buyer pays premium to seller (writer). In the money: call when stock > strike; put when stock < strike. At the money: stock = strike. Out of the money: call when stock < strike; put when stock > strike. Intrinsic value + time value = premium. American options: exercise any time before expiration. European options: exercise only at expiration. Options not appropriate for all investors — suitability required.

Options Strategies

Long call: bullish, max loss = premium. Long put: bearish, max gain = strike - premium. Covered call: own stock + sell call = generates income, caps upside. Protective put: own stock + buy put = insurance against decline. Short call: bearish/neutral, max gain = premium, unlimited risk. Short put: bullish/neutral. Married put = protective put on newly purchased stock. Straddle: buy call + put at same strike = profits from big move either direction.

Variable Annuities & Insurance Products

Fixed annuity: guaranteed return, in general account. Variable annuity: performance-based, in separate account (like a mutual fund), owner bears investment risk. Accumulation units (during growth phase) → annuity units (during payout). Death benefit: typically returns greater of account value or amount paid in. Surrender charges: apply if early withdrawal. Variable annuities are securities — require securities license. Fixed annuities are insurance products only. 1035 exchange: tax-free swap of one annuity/life policy for another.

Alternative & Packaged Products

REITs: must distribute 90% of taxable income as dividends, pass-through tax treatment, highly liquid (exchange-traded). DPPs (Direct Participation Programs): LPs, oil/gas, real estate — illiquid, pass-through tax losses, accredited investors, long lock-up. Hedge funds: private, unregistered, only qualified/accredited investors, very illiquid. Private equity: buy/restructure private companies, 10+ year lock-ups. Variable life insurance: cash value tied to separate account performance, death benefit may fluctuate.

Key Risk Types (Must Know All)

Market risk: overall market decline. Credit/default risk: issuer fails to pay. Interest rate risk: bond prices fall when rates rise. Inflation (purchasing power) risk: returns don't keep pace with inflation (biggest risk for fixed-income long-term holders). Reinvestment risk: coupon payments reinvested at lower rates when rates fall. Call risk: issuer calls bond when rates fall (hurts investor). Liquidity risk: can't sell quickly at fair price. Currency risk: foreign investments lose value from exchange rates. Legislative/regulatory risk: law changes hurt investment.

Domain 3 — Trading, Customer Accounts & Prohibited Activities
31% · ~23 questions▶
Order Types

Market order: execute immediately at best available price (guaranteed execution, not price). Limit order: execute only at specified price or better (guaranteed price, not execution). Stop order: becomes market order when stock hits stop price — used to limit losses or protect gains. Stop-limit: becomes limit order (not market) at trigger price — not guaranteed to execute. Day order: expires end of day. GTC (Good-til-Cancelled): stays open until filled or cancelled. AON (All-or-None): fill entire order or none. FOK (Fill or Kill): fill immediately and completely or cancel.

Trade Settlement

Equities (stocks): T+1 (as of May 28, 2024 — SEC mandated change from T+2). Options: T+1. Municipal bonds: T+2. Corporate bonds: T+2. U.S. Treasuries: T+1. Cash settlement: same day (rare). Ex-dividend date: buy on or after = no dividend. Record date: must be on books as shareholder. Payment date: when dividend actually paid. Settlement terminology: regular way = standard settlement per security type.

Margin Accounts & Regulation T

Reg T: initial margin requirement = 50% for stock purchases and short sales (set by Federal Reserve). FINRA maintenance margin: minimum 25% equity for long positions, 30% for short positions. Margin call triggered when equity falls below maintenance. Minimum to open margin account: $2,000. Options: Reg T does NOT permit margin on option purchases — must be paid in full. Short selling: must be in margin account; borrow shares from broker, sell, hope price drops.

Customer Account Types

Individual: one owner, one decision maker. Joint JTWROS (Joint Tenants With Right of Survivorship): deceased's share passes automatically to survivor(s). Joint TIC (Tenants in Common): deceased's share passes to estate (not survivor). Corporate accounts: authorized by corporate resolution. Trust accounts: managed per trust document. UGMA/UTMA: custodial accounts for minors — one custodian, one minor, irrevocable gift, becomes minor's property at majority (18–21). Fiduciary accounts: managed on behalf of another (guardian, executor, trustee).

New Account & KYC Requirements

Know Your Customer (KYC): FINRA Rule 2090. Must obtain: name, address, DOB, SSN/TIN, employment status, financial situation, investment objectives, risk tolerance. Beneficial ownership: for legal entity accounts, must identify all owners with 25%+ control. Form U4: registered rep's registration application. Customer account form must be signed. Suitability: recommendations must match customer's investment profile. Reg BI (Regulation Best Interest): BD must act in retail customer's BEST INTEREST, not just suitable.

Prohibited Practices — Market Manipulation

Front-running: trading for firm's own account AHEAD of large customer orders. Churning/excessive trading: excessive transactions primarily to generate commissions. Painting the tape / wash trading: fake transactions to create appearance of trading activity. Pump and dump: spreading false positive information to inflate price, then selling. Marking the close: executing trades at day's end to manipulate closing price. Backing away: market maker refuses to honor quoted price. Spoofing: placing fake orders with intent to cancel before execution.

Insider Trading & Information Barriers

Material non-public information (MNPI): any info a reasonable investor would consider significant AND that is not public. Trading on MNPI = securities fraud. Tippers AND tippees can both be prosecuted (not just the trader). Chinese walls / information barriers: policies to prevent MNPI from flowing between departments (e.g., investment banking and trading). Misappropriation theory: using confidential info in breach of a duty = insider trading even if not a company insider.

Anti-Money Laundering (AML)

Bank Secrecy Act (BSA): foundation of AML law. Currency Transaction Report (CTR): required for cash transactions exceeding $10,000 — filed with FinCEN. Suspicious Activity Report (SAR): required for suspicious transactions of $5,000 or more — filed with FinCEN within 30 days of detection. Structuring (smurfing): breaking up transactions to avoid $10,000 threshold — illegal itself. OFAC: Office of Foreign Assets Control — must screen customers against SDN (Specially Designated Nationals) list. CIP (Customer Identification Program): verify customer identity when opening accounts.

Corporate Actions

Stock split (forward): shares increase, price decreases proportionally (2:1 → 100 shares @ $50 becomes 200 @ $25). Reverse split: shares decrease, price increases (reduces shareholders). Cash dividend: taxable in year paid. Stock dividend: additional shares issued, reduces per-share price proportionally, not taxable at receipt. Rights offering: existing shareholders offered right to buy new shares at discount (preemptive rights). Spin-off: company divides into separate companies. Tender offer: acquiring company offers to buy shares directly from shareholders at premium.

Other Prohibited Practices

Selling away: registered rep sells products NOT offered by their broker-dealer without firm approval. Unauthorized trading: executing trades without customer authorization in non-discretionary accounts. Misrepresentation: false or misleading statements about securities. Guaranteeing against loss: prohibited — can never promise a customer they won't lose money. Sharing in customer accounts: prohibited unless registered rep has written customer AND firm approval, and shares proportional to contribution. Free-riding: buying and selling before paying (violates Reg T).

Domain 4 — Overview of the Regulatory Framework
9% · ~7 questions▶
Key Securities Laws

Securities Act of 1933 ("Truth in Securities Act"): regulates NEW securities offerings — requires registration with SEC and prospectus delivery. Securities Exchange Act of 1934: created the SEC, regulates SECONDARY market trading, broker-dealers, exchanges. Investment Company Act of 1940: regulates mutual funds and other investment companies. Investment Advisers Act of 1940: regulates investment advisers. SIPC Act of 1970: created SIPC to protect customer assets at failed broker-dealers. Sarbanes-Oxley 2002: corporate governance reforms post-Enron.

SIPC Protection

Protects customers of FAILED broker-dealers (not against market losses!). Covers up to $500,000 per customer account, with a $250,000 limit on cash claims. Separate SIPC coverage for: individual accounts, joint accounts, IRA accounts — each treated separately. SIPC does NOT cover: commodity futures, currency, investment contracts not registered as securities. Funded by member assessments (all FINRA member firms must be SIPC members).

Regulatory Bodies: SEC, FINRA, MSRB

SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission): federal regulator, oversees ALL securities markets, administers securities laws, enforcement authority. FINRA (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority): SRO that regulates broker-dealers and registered reps. Writes rules, conducts exams, enforces. Funded by industry. MSRB (Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board): writes rules for municipal securities, but has NO direct enforcement power — FINRA enforces for BDs, SEC enforces for munis. SRO rules may be STRICTER than SEC rules, never less strict.

Registration & Licensing

Associated persons at FINRA member firms must be registered. Registration requires passing SIE + appropriate top-off exam (Series 6, 7, 57, etc.). Form U4: uniform registration application — must update within 30 days of material changes. Form U5: filed when registered person leaves a firm. Continuing education (CE): Regulatory Element (FINRA-mandated, online, within 1 year of initial registration then every 3 years), Firm Element (firm-administered, annually). NASAA: North American Securities Administrators Association — state-level securities regulation.

Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI)

Effective June 2020. Applies to broker-dealers making recommendations to RETAIL CUSTOMERS only. Four components: Care obligation (act in customer's best interest), Conflict of interest obligation (identify and mitigate conflicts), Disclosure obligation (Form CRS), Compliance obligation. Investment advisers are held to a higher FIDUCIARY standard (must put client interest ahead of own at all times). Form CRS (Customer Relationship Summary): short document explaining relationship, services, fees — must be provided to retail investors.

Test-taking strategy

Process of elimination: Even if unsure, eliminate 2 wrong answers first. Odds jump from 25% to 50%.

"Most likely" / "best describes": The SIE loves these qualifiers. All answers may have some truth — pick the BEST one.

Flag & skip: You have ~80 seconds per question. Don't dwell — flag and return. Never leave blank (no penalty for guessing).

Watch for "EXCEPT" / "NOT": These questions are traps. Slow down and underline the negative word.

Changing answers: Only change if you have a specific, concrete reason. First instinct is usually right.

Suitability questions: Match the investor's profile — young + aggressive = growth/equity; retired + conservative = bonds/income; high earner = municipals for tax benefit.

Hawkish vs. Dovish (must know)

Hawkish (FRB raising rates): bond prices fall, investors should shorten duration, borrowing gets expensive, growth stocks hurt, banks benefit.

Dovish (FRB lowering rates): bond prices rise, longer duration bonds benefit most, growth stocks boosted, borrowing cheaper, housing market stimulated.

Bond yield order when trading at premium or discount

Premium bond (price > par): Coupon Rate > Current Yield > YTM > YTC

Discount bond (price < par): Coupon Rate < Current Yield < YTM < YTC

Memory trick: For premiums, all yields decrease from coupon. For discounts, all yields increase from coupon.

Suitability quick reference

High tax bracket investor: Municipal bonds (tax-free federal interest)

Safety-first investor: U.S. Treasury securities (no default risk)

Income + some growth: Convertible bonds

Inflation hedge: TIPS, equities, real assets

Aggressive growth: Common stock, growth funds

Never put munis in an IRA: Wasting the tax advantage on an already tax-deferred account
